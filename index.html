9 films / 3 decades / 4 nations

Anywhere But Now

cinema beyond linear reality
CURATOR
Naumaan Faridi
§ 01 INTRODUCTION

Movies touch our hearts and awaken our vision, and change the way we see things. They take us to other places, they open doors and minds.

— Martin Scorsese

Many say that Cinema can sometimes be a time machine. Ever since the Lumière brothers projected a train pulling into the station and audiences got startled as if the future had already arrived, Cinema has been a window that lets us see into the past and sometimes even the future. A film is able to convey someone’s entire lifetime in just 90 minutes, replay a historical event from over a hundred years ago, or even position you into a year that has not yet arrived. Cinema does not just record time as it happens. It reshapes time into something we feel.

A brief history of cinematic time

The theme of time travel in films dates back to early cinema, specifically to The Ghost of Slumber Mountain (1917), which used a magical “looking glass” to depict the prehistoric past. However, the idea is older than film itself, with H.G. Wells publishing The Time Machine in 1895, the same year cinema was born. Later films like A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur’s Court and The Time Machine helped shape time travel into a recognizable idea in science fiction. By the late twentieth century, films such as Planet of the Apes, The Terminator, and Back to the Future made time travel a popular and flexible theme that almost anyone keeping up with pop culture was familiar with. Over time, time travel stories evolved from simple journeys into the past or future into more complicated stories about alternate timelines, loops, memory, consequences, destiny, and emotional impact. This flexibility of time travel will be explored in this film festival.

Defining characteristics of Time Travel in films

Time travel films usually focus on characters moving outside normal time. This can happen through machines, portals, accidents, superpowers, magic, or complex science. The genre often includes recurring ideas like changing the past, preventing a bad future, meeting another version of yourself, getting stuck in a loop, or accidentally creating a paradox. Visually and narratively, these films often cut between different time periods, repeat scenes with some sort of changes each time, or slowly reveal how events are closely intertwined. In these films even while the story may get confusing, the main tension of the film comes from one timeless question: Can the character change what happened, or is their fate sealed no matter what they change?

Cultural Significance

Time travel matters because it connects science fiction to emotions that almost everyone feels. Most people have at least one thing in their past they regret or wish they could change. Similarly, we often worry about the future, whether we will succeed, what kind of life we will have, or what the world will become. In real life, time travel is not possible, but film makes it possible to imagine those questions in a visual and emotional way. The theme opens up a new perspective on life by asking what would happen if people could revisit the past, alter the future, or see the consequences of their choices before it is too late. This gives time travel films a whole new realm to explore, not just through science fiction, but through emotion, memory, fate, and human desire.

The films in Anywhere But Now are not united by genre. Anywhere But Now looks at nine films from different countries and decades that explore the theme of time travel. The festival shows how the theme can be incorporated in different ways to achieve different results depending on the story being told. Sometimes it becomes a way to deal with love, regret, and memory. Other times, it creates loops, paradoxes, or grand spectacle. By bringing these films together, the festival shows how much weight the theme of time travel can carry in storytelling and how it lets audiences imagine life from a perspective that reality does not allow.

A
Category A

Personal & Emotional Time

Two films where time is not a machine but a feeling. A man's story about cherishing little things. A woman's story about learning to accept fate. These films use nonlinear time to ask what we owe the people we love, and what memory is for.

01
Category A · PERSONAL & EMOTIONAL

About Time

Director Richard Curtis Year 2013 Country United Kingdom Runtime 123 min
SYNOPSIS

On the day of Tim’s 21st birthday, his father informs him that every man in their family is capable of traveling back in time to any point in their past. He utilizes this power just like any other individual would, ineffectively, romantically, and to chase after a young woman whom he can’t forget. This movie, which starts off as an appealing British comedy, gradually shifts gears to become reflective about those mundane moments in life that we never take into account. While his power allows Tim to undo embarrassing encounters and wrongdoings, it does not prevent the passage of time for his loved ones. The film asks whether the point of time travel might be to not change things at all.

WHY THIS FILM

Most time travel films treat the device as an engine for plot. About Time treats it as an engine for feeling. This film fits perfectly in this category because instead of following the typical epic spectacle associated with time travel, it instead asks the only question that actually matters: if you could live a moment again, would you change it, or would you simply be more present inside it? We have all looked back on the "good old days" in retrospect and realized that instead of worrying about the future maybe we shouldn't have taken what we had for granted. This film is a reminder that nonlinear time is not always about saving the world. Sometimes it is about a wedding speech, or a walk on the beach, or one more conversation with someone who is gone.

Fun Fact
  • Richard Curtis has said the film is less about time travel than about how to live an ordinary life well. He calls it his most personal screenplay.
  • This is one of four movies in which Rachel McAdams stars as the love interest of a time traveler.
02
Category A · PERSONAL & EMOTIONAL

Arrival

Director Denis Villeneuve Year 2016 Country United States Runtime 116 min
SYNOPSIS

Twelve different space ships land in different parts of the Earth, and the government wants to figure out why they are here. They are not here to attack. They are not here to announce themselves. They merely hang, watching and waiting. Linguist Louise Banks is called upon by the armed forces to achieve what is deemed impossible. She must learn the language of aliens with nothing in common and nothing in context to work with, as the nations of the earth inch closer and closer to global war. As Louise gains a grasp of their script, something begins to change about how she perceives time itself.

WHY THIS FILM

If About Time is the festival's heart, Arrival is its thesis statement. It is a very unique film where the structure of the storytelling is the argument about time itself. Louise does not travel to the past or future. She experiences her life as a single unfolding shape. The film argues, subtly and devastatingly, that knowing what comes next does not free us from grief, and it is the very essence that makes love meaningful. The films emphasizes that no matter how hard we may grasp at controlling our life and the future, true peace comes from embracing the experience of life rather than trying to control or change the outcome.

Fun Fact
  • The Heptapod language was designed by a team that included a real linguist. The hundred plus logograms were treated as an actual writing system, with internal grammar.
  • Composer Jóhann Jóhannsson's score uses overlapping vocal samples to create the auditory sensation of time folding in on itself. It was the last major score he completed before his death.
B
Category B

Time Loops & Repetition

This category shows time loops as one of the most intense forms of time travel. Instead of simply visiting another time, characters are trapped inside repetition, forced to keep learning, changing, and carrying memories that no one else has.

03
Category B · TIME LOOPS

Edge of Tomorrow

Director Doug Liman Year 2014 Country United States Runtime 113 min
SYNOPSIS

Major William Cage is a military spokesman who has never seen combat. When he is demoted and abruptly dropped into the front line of a losing war against an alien force, he dies in the first five minutes. Then he wakes up. Then he dies again. Trapped in a loop of the same brutal day, Cage trains, fails, and tries again, with the help of a battle hardened soldier who has lived through this nightmare before. The film treats the loop as a video game, a training montage, and eventually, something close to a love story. This film is a time travel to save the world and Cage is responsible for locating the "Omega" (the alien brain) and winning the war.

WHY THIS FILM

Edge of Tomorrow belongs in the Time Loops & Repetition category because it turns time travel into a process of failure, learning, and growth. William Cage is forced to relive the same battle again and again, but each loop gives him another chance to understand the enemy, improve himself, and change the outcome. What makes the film work is that he does not give up, even when the repetition becomes exhausting and painful. He keeps trying to figure out what went wrong and how to protect the people around him. This makes the time loop more than just an action gimmick. It becomes a story about persistence, courage, and learning through failure. The movie shows how time travel can create both grand spectacle and personal transformation.

Fun Fact
  • The film bombed at the US box office under its cryptic title, then was rebranded for home release as Live Die Repeat. It is now widely regarded as one of the most underrated science fiction films of the 2010s.
  • Emily Blunt trained for months with a real military combat coach. She has said the suit she wore weighed roughly 85 pounds.
04
Category B · TIME LOOPS

Steins;Gate (シュタインズ・ゲート)

Directors Hiroshi Hamasaki, Takuya Satō Year 2013 Country Japan Runtime 90 minutes
SYNOPSIS

With the conclusion of the anime, the "Steins Gate" timeline has finally found its peace. But the protagonist, Rintaro Okabe, is now suffering from some unusual after effects of his past escapades into different timelines. His brain is being bombarded with memories of alternate realities, making him disappear physically in brief instances. With Okabe disappearing in front of her eyes, as well as everyone else forgetting that he existed, Kurisu Makise finds herself unable to shake the feeling of sorrow for someone she doesn't even know. To rescue the one who rescued her, she must take charge and venture into the risks of time travel on her own.

WHY THIS FILM

This festival would be incomplete without animation, and Steins;Gate: The Movie shows how time travel can become deeply psychological and emotional. It belongs in the Time Loops & Repetition category because it explores what it means to remember events that no one else does. The film is emotional, but its main structure is still about looping, remembering, and dealing with the consequences of time being changed again and again. Unlike Edge of Tomorrow, which uses repetition for action and survival, Steins;Gate uses it to focus on the mental and emotional cost of changing time. It's a story about remembering everything while forgetting yourself. Its inclusion also expands the festival beyond Western cinema, showing how Japanese animation can handle time travel with the same seriousness and complexity as live-action cinema.

Fun Fact
  • The anime has been credited with creating an entire subgenre of "serious" anime time loop storytelling that influenced everything from Re:Zero to Erased.
C
Category C

Paradox & Complex Time

This category shows time travel at its most complicated. What happens when the future shapes the past? Can a person still choose their path if time already seems written? These films turn time into a puzzle that is both exciting and unsettling.

05
Category C · PARADOX

Interstellar

Director Christopher Nolan Year 2014 Country United States Runtime 169 min
SYNOPSIS

Earth is dying. Crops are failing, dust covers everything, and humanity may only have one generation left. Cooper, a former pilot, is chosen for a secret mission to travel through a wormhole near Saturn and search for a new home for humankind. But the mission comes with a painful cost. Time does not move the same way in deep space, and every hour Cooper spends near a black hole means years passing for his daughter back on Earth. Underneath all of its scientific concepts, Interstellar is really a film about a father trying to keep his promise to his daughter.

WHY THIS FILM

Interstellar belongs in Paradox & Complex Time because it treats time as something shaped by science, not just imagination. The film explores how gravity, distance, and space can change the way time is experienced. What feels like hours for one person can become years for someone else, and that difference becomes one of the film’s biggest conflicts. This makes the story both complex and emotional, because the science directly affects Cooper’s relationship with his daughter. Interstellar fits this category because it shows time travel as more than simply moving to the past or future. It becomes a difficult force that shapes sacrifice, survival, and the hope of saving humanity.

Fun Fact
  • The film's black hole, Gargantua, was rendered using equations co developed by physicist Kip Thorne. The visual effects team published a peer reviewed paper on what they discovered.
  • The corn field shown in the film was real. Nolan's team grew over 500 acres of corn, then sold the harvest to recoup costs.
06
Category C · PARADOX

Tenet

Director Christopher Nolan Year 2020 Country United States / United Kingdom Runtime 150 min
SYNOPSIS

An unnamed CIA operative known only as the Protagonist is recruited into a shadow war involving objects whose entropy runs backward. Bullets fly into guns. Cars uncrash. As he is pulled deeper into the conspiracy, the Protagonist learns that the conflict he is fighting is not happening in the order he is experiencing it. The film unfolds as a palindrome, and the second half rewards the viewer who paid attention to the first. It is dense, demanding, and for some difficult to get through. It is one of the most ambitious uses of practical effects in twenty first century cinema.

WHY THIS FILM

Tenet belongs in Paradox & Complex Time because it is the festival’s most extreme experiment with time. Instead of simply jumping to the past or future, the film imagines time as something that can move forward and backward at once. This makes cause and effect feel unstable, turning the story and action scenes into a puzzle the audience has to learn while watching. The film fits this category because it treats time travel as both a narrative idea and a structural challenge. Some viewers may find it confusing, while others may find it exciting, but that reaction is part of what makes it worth including. Tenet gives the festival a film that invites debate about how far time travel storytelling can be pushed.

Fun Fact
  • For the airport sequence, the production crashed an actual decommissioned Boeing 747 into a hangar. Nolan judged this cheaper than convincing visual effects.
  • The cast were given partial scripts and asked to perform some scenes both forward and backward, then play them in reverse. Robert Pattinson has said he is still not sure he understands the plot.
07
Category C · PARADOX

Predestination

Directors Michael & Peter Spierig Year 2014 Country Australia Runtime 97 min
SYNOPSIS

In 1970s New York, a bartender listens as a stranger tells him one of the strangest life stories imaginable. What the stranger does not know is that the bartender is secretly a temporal agent on his final mission, chasing a terrorist across different decades. As their stories unfold, the connection between them becomes stranger and harder to explain. Saying too much would ruin the experience, because Predestination is built around surprise, discovery, and careful reveals. The film starts small, with two people talking in a bar, but it slowly grows into something much larger. Its real power comes from how every detail begins to matter. Predestination is a small film in terms of budget and scale, but its ideas about time, identity, and fate are huge.

WHY THIS FILM

Predestination belongs in Paradox & Complex Time because it takes the logic of time travel further than almost any other film in the festival. Instead of using time travel for spectacle, it builds a tight mystery around identity, fate, and cause and effect. The film keeps folding its story back onto itself, making the audience question where events truly begin and whether the characters are making choices or simply following a path that has already been written. This makes it a strong fit for the category because its time travel is not just part of the plot. It is the entire structure of the film. Predestination shows how complex time travel can turn a small, intimate story into something much bigger and more unsettling.

Fun Fact
  • Sarah Snook spent up to four hours a day in prosthetic makeup. Many viewers on first watch did not realize she was playing the role at all.
  • When Ethan Hawke looks at his watch (8 mins 18 seconds in), his real birthday is shown.
  • The Spierig brothers shot the film in Melbourne, recreating period New York with a fraction of the budget Hollywood would have spent.
D
Category D

Blockbuster Time Travel

Time travel as entertainment, comedy, and rescue mission. These two films are separated by 34 years, but both show how nonlinear storytelling can become pure blockbuster fun. The festival closes here on purpose, because no films have shaped mainstream ideas of time travel more clearly than these two.

08
Category D · BLOCKBUSTER

Back to the Future

Director Robert Zemeckis Year 1985 Country United States Runtime 116 min
SYNOPSIS

Marty McFly is a 17-year-old guitar player whose best friend, Doc Brown, is a strange but brilliant inventor. Doc has managed to turn a stainless steel sports car into a working time machine, but when things go wrong, Marty is sent back to 1955. There, he accidentally interferes with the moment his parents are supposed to fall in love. Now Marty has to figure out how to get them together, protect his own future, and make it back home without revealing who he really is. Back to the Future is fast, funny, and carefully built, with small details early in the film paying off later in clever ways.

WHY THIS FILM

Almost every blockbuster time travel film made since 1985 is in conversation with this one, whether it knows it or not. The film takes a complicated idea and makes it funny, fast, and easy to follow without losing the stakes. Marty’s trip to 1955 becomes more than a science fiction adventure. It becomes a comedy, a family story, and a coming-of-age film at the same time. Including it gives the festival an important 1980s anchor and shows how time travel can be both clever and deeply entertaining. It is also one of the most accessible examples of how one small change in the past can reshape an entire future.

Fun Fact
  • The DeLorean was chosen because the screenwriters wanted a vehicle that would plausibly be mistaken for a UFO when seen by 1955 farmers.
  • A Universal executive pushed hard to retitle the film Spaceman from Pluto, believing audiences would not see a movie with the word "future" in the title. Steven Spielberg killed the idea by sarcastically thanking him for the "joke" memo.
09
Category D · BLOCKBUSTER

Avengers: Endgame

Directors Anthony & Joe Russo Year 2019 Country United States Runtime 181 min
SYNOPSIS

Half of all life in the universe has disappeared, and the remaining Avengers are left in a quieter, more broken world. They come together to search for one last chance to fix what happened, and that chance comes through time travel. The film becomes a heist across the franchise’s own past, sending the heroes back into familiar moments, places, and versions of themselves that audiences already know. Avengers: Endgame does not just use time travel as a science fiction trick. It uses it to revisit more than a decade of connected films and give those stories a sense of closure.

WHY THIS FILM

Including Avengers: Endgame is intentional because this festival is not only interested in “serious” time travel films. It also looks at what time travel becomes when it is used inside one of the biggest pop culture franchises ever made. Endgame belongs in Blockbuster Time Travel because its “time heist” turns the franchise’s own past into part of the story. The characters do not just travel through time. They travel through moments the audience already remembers, which makes nostalgia feel like a setting. Placing it next to Back to the Future also shows how much blockbuster time travel changed over 34 years, from one teenager’s accidental trip to the past to a massive film built to close an entire saga.

Fun Fact
  • The film became, briefly, the highest grossing film of all time, surpassing Avatar before being overtaken again. It earned roughly 2.7 billion dollars worldwide.
  • The film openly mocks Back to the Future's time travel rules in dialogue, then proceeds to use a different model entirely.
